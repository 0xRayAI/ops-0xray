Multi-agent operations

One plane, one queue

Yes. You get a multi-agent system that ships on schedule when we put ownership, review gates, spend, and audit on one agentic control plane. Vendors such as IBM, Snowflake, and Microsoft now describe the control plane as its own layer; AI Fleet OPS sets that layer up across whatever frameworks you already run.

Why does an AI fleet need one control plane?

Most teams start with one agent, then add a second for a different job, then a third on a different framework. Each one logs somewhere different, spends on its own key, and hands off work in chat. Microsoft's product page calls this collection an "AI fleet", and IBM pitches "one control plane for AI agents" wherever they were built. The idea is simple: one place that knows who owns each job, what it costs, and whether it passed review.

What does AI Fleet OPS actually do?

We wire the operating layer — roles, a shared work queue, review gates before anything merges or goes public, and spend tracking per agent. The open-source 0xRay suit is how we carry that layer (in our own stack we call each agent's kit a suit; that is gloss, not a requirement).

What else do operators ask?

Is a control plane the same as an orchestration framework?

No. Snowflake separates control plane, orchestration, and observability; frameworks mostly handle orchestration.

Do I have to replace my framework?

No. The control plane sits over it.

Is this a product or a service?

AI Fleet OPS is consulting on top of the open-source 0xRay suit — Scout, Stand-up, or Retain.

How does a consult work?

The manual is the work. A consult is how we run that work with you. You keep the keys.

Scout

One session. A map of roles, queue, gates, and the gaps that will drop the job on the next restart.

Stand-up

House rules, one queue, the kit fastened, and the first release gate on a real ticket.

Retain

Weekly operations on the queue and the gates. The public lane opens only when you call it.
